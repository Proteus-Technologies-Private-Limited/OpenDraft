Script Statistics & Timing

Analyze your screenplay with charts, dialogue breakdowns, and runtime estimates.

Opening Script Statistics

Open the statistics panel from Tools > Script Statistics in the menu bar. The panel replaces the editor area with a full-width analytics dashboard. Click the × button in the top-right to close it and return to your screenplay.

💡

Live data: All statistics are computed directly from your screenplay content. They update automatically as you write — no manual refresh needed.

Overview Cards

Four summary cards appear at the top of the panel:

CardShows
PagesTotal page count and estimated runtime
ScenesTotal scene count and average scene length (in pages)
CharactersNumber of speaking characters and total dialogue lines
WordsTotal word count and average words per page

Writing Goals, Sprints & Words Written

OpenDraft keeps track of how much you write each day and can hold you to a goal. Open Tools > Writing Goals & Sprints…, or click the progress ring in the status bar.

  • Daily goal — words to write each day. The status bar ring fills as you go and turns green when you reach it.
  • Session goal — words or pages to write since you opened OpenDraft. The ring shows it when there is no daily goal.
  • Sprints — pick a length (5 to 60 minutes) and start. The status bar counts down and shows the words written so far; you are told the total when time is up. Tick No backspace to keep moving forward: Backspace and Delete do nothing until the sprint ends.
  • Words Written in Script Statistics shows a calendar of the last 18 weeks — darker means more words — with today’s total, the last seven days and your current streak.

Words are counted from your scripts a few seconds after you stop typing. Only additions count — deleting does not take words away — and opening a script, importing one or pasting a large block of text is not counted as writing. In a shared session, a pause in which a collaborator also edited is skipped, so their words are not counted as yours. The history stays on this device and is never sent anywhere.

Dialogue Distribution

A horizontal bar chart shows how dialogue is distributed across your characters, sorted by word count. Each bar is color-coded using the character's assigned highlight color from the Characters panel.

Below the chart, a detailed table lists every speaking character with:

  • Lines — Number of dialogue lines
  • Words — Total dialogue word count
  • % Dialogue — Percentage of total screenplay dialogue
  • Scenes — Number of scenes the character appears in
  • Role — The character's role (Lead, Supporting, etc.) from their profile
💡

Balance check: If a supporting character has more dialogue than your protagonist, it might signal a structural issue — or that they deserve a bigger role.

Gender Analysis

A donut chart shows the dialogue split by character gender, based on the gender field in each character's profile. An accompanying table breaks down the numbers by gender group:

  • Characters — Number of characters of that gender
  • Lines — Total dialogue lines
  • Words — Total dialogue words
  • % — Percentage of total dialogue

Characters without a gender assigned appear under “Unassigned”. To get accurate results, fill in the gender field in your character profiles.

Scene Breakdown

Four mini-charts in a 2×2 grid give you a bird’s-eye view of your screenplay’s structure:

ChartWhat it shows
Interior / ExteriorPie chart of INT. vs EXT. vs INT./EXT. scenes
Time of DayPie chart of Day vs Night vs Other scenes
Scene Length DistributionBar chart showing how many scenes fall into each length bucket (<1 page, 1–2 pages, 2–3 pages, 3–5 pages, 5+ pages)
Top LocationsBar chart of the most frequently used locations

Pacing Chart

A stacked area chart shows dialogue versus action density across your screenplay, scene by scene. The x-axis represents each scene in order, and the y-axis shows word count.

  • Blue areas represent dialogue-heavy sections
  • Orange areas represent action-heavy sections

This visualization shows the “rhythm” of your screenplay. A healthy script typically alternates between dialogue and action, creating peaks and valleys. Long flat stretches of a single color may indicate pacing issues.

Character Presence Map

A grid/heatmap where each row is a character (sorted by total appearances) and each column is a scene. Filled cells indicate the character appears in that scene.

This visualization helps you answer questions like:

  • Does my protagonist disappear for long stretches?
  • Do any characters appear in only one scene (and should they be cut)?
  • Are there scenes with too many or too few characters?

Reports

Go to Tools > Analytics > Reports… for the script as tables a production can work from. Pick a report on the left to preview it, then Export CSV… for a spreadsheet or a scheduling program, or Export PDF… to print or share.

ReportWhat it lists
Scene ReportEvery scene: number, heading, interior or exterior, location, time of day, length in eighths of a page, estimated screen time, who speaks and its word count
Character ReportEvery speaking character: role and gender from their profile, speeches, words, their share of the dialogue, and the scenes they speak in
Location ReportEvery location: interior or exterior, the times of day it is used, how many scenes and pages, and which scenes
Cast ReportEvery character with a profile or a line: role, gender, age, speaking scenes and description
Breakdown ReportEvery tagged item by category — props, costumes, vehicles and the rest — with the scenes it appears in and its notes
Notes ReportEvery note in script order: its scene, the text it is on, who wrote it, the note, whether it is open or resolved (and by whom), who it is assigned to and when it was written. Each reply is a row under its note, numbered 1.1, 1.2…. General notes come after the script notes.
Open NotesThe same, for only the notes not yet resolved: what is still to do

The CSV opens in Excel, Numbers and Google Sheets with accented names intact. Plugins can add reports of their own to the list.

Script Timing

OpenDraft estimates how long your screenplay will take to screen using the industry rule of thumb — one printed page is about one minute — measured on the content of each page rather than a word count:

  • Every element is wrapped at its own column width, the way it prints: dialogue’s narrow column takes more lines than the same words as action.
  • The white space counts: the blank lines above scene headings, action and character cues are screen time too.
  • Character cues, scene headings and transitions each take their own line.
  • The title page, Sections and Notes are not on the clock.

Lines are converted at your page size (about 54–58 lines a page) and the formatting template’s page time — 60 seconds for a screenplay, 30 for a multi-camera sitcom. The estimate is of story time: it does not include end credits.

Genre & Pacing

A page can’t show how a film will be directed. Open Format → Genre & Pacing… (or click Est. in the status bar) to tell OpenDraft:

PacingEffectFor
Brisk×0.85Fast-talking, quick cutting, overlapping dialogue
Standard×1One page ≈ one minute — right for most scripts (the default)
Measured×1.3Slow-burn or contemplative — long silences and lingering images

Each option shows the runtime it would give your script. Pacing never changes a scene timing you have set by hand.

In the same dialog you can tag the script with genres — pick any of the suggestions and add your own. Genres are saved with the script but don’t change the runtime: tested against released films, genre on its own said nothing about how long a script plays.

Runtime in the Status Bar

The estimated total runtime is always visible in the status bar at the bottom of the editor (e.g., “Est. 1h 47m 12s”, or “Est. 2h 5m 40s · measured” when a pacing other than Standard is set). It updates as you write; click it to change the pacing.

Per-Scene Timing

In the Scene Navigator, each scene shows a timing badge next to the page-length indicator. The badge is color-coded:

  • Grey — Under 1 minute (very short scene)
  • Green — 1–3 minutes (normal length)
  • Yellow — 3–5 minutes (long scene)
  • Red — Over 5 minutes (very long scene)

Timing Overrides

Some scenes may be shorter or longer than the algorithm estimates — for example, a montage covering three pages might only take 30 seconds of screen time. You can set a manual timing override on any scene heading to replace the automatic estimate. The override is stored as an attribute on the scene heading and persists with your screenplay.

Timing Report

At the bottom of the Script Statistics panel, the Timing Report table shows every scene with:

ColumnDescription
#Scene number
SceneScene heading text
DialogueEstimated dialogue duration
ActionEstimated action duration
Est.Total estimated duration (with override indicator if set)
CumulativeRunning total from the start of the screenplay

The final row shows the total estimated runtime for the entire screenplay.

ℹ️

Industry context: Feature films typically run 90–120 minutes (90–120 pages). TV hour-long episodes run 45–60 minutes. The timing report helps you hit your target length before you hand off the script.
